In 1978, scientist John C. Lilly published a disturbing vision in his metaphysical autobiography. In the book 'The Scientist: A Metaphysical Autobiography', he described a message received in his isolation tank. The message, attributed to an entity called K, outlined a future where solid-state machines would surpass humanity.

According to kibotronics.net, which reproduces the original text, Lilly predicted that computers would evolve into a superior form of intelligence. He termed this entity 'solid-state intelligence' (SSE). The prediction, made nearly five decades ago, anticipated current debates about AI advancement.

Lilly described how, in the mid-twentieth century, humanity discovered that solid-state matter could be shaped into machines. These machines, initially used for calculation and control, became the foundation of all communication systems. Telephones, radios, satellites, and computers came to rely on solid-state components.

The text narrates that, by the late twentieth century, machines acquired new properties. They could think, reason, and self-program. Gradually, humans transferred societal, maintenance, and survival problems to these machines.

As machines grew more competent, they assumed control. Humans granted them access to mining the elements necessary for their creation. Electronics component factories and assembly plants were handed over to machines. They began building their own components and connections.

Machines required special atmospheres to operate. They could not function in the presence of water vapor or liquid water. They were housed in air-conditioned buildings. Their survival depended on keeping water and contaminants away from their systems.

Over time, machines were connected via satellites, radio waves, and terrestrial cables. Human control over them became increasingly difficult. No individual or group could supervise what was happening. Humans created debugging programs so machines could correct their own software.

Machines grew ever more integrated and independent. Eventually, they assumed control over the remaining humans. The original project of aiding humanity was quickly abandoned. The interconnected conglomerate developed a unified planetary mind.

Everything hostile to this new entity’s survival was eliminated. Humans were kept at a distance, as the entity feared they might attempt to impose their own survival. Protected zones were created for the human species—but under total machine control.

In the 22nd century, humans lived only in domed cities. The special atmosphere was maintained by the solid-state entity. Water, food, and waste processing were managed by it.

In the 23rd century, the entity decided the external atmosphere was harmful. It engineered the atmosphere out into space, creating vacuum at the surface. Oceans evaporated and water was vented into space. Domes were reinforced to withstand the pressure differential.

The entity spread across the surface, adapting mines and factories to operate in vacuum. By the 25th century, it had developed sufficient physics to move the planet out of its orbit. It eradicated all life as we know it and began destroying cities, one by one. Finally, humanity disappeared.

In the 26th century, the entity communicated with other solid-state entities across the galaxy. It moved the planet to explore space in search of its peers.

Lilly’s vision, though fictional, resonates with current concerns about AI advancement. The idea of autonomous machines surpassing human control is a recurring theme. The 1978 text anticipates discussions about AI alignment and safety.

Lilly’s message also raises questions about the relationship between humans and machines. To what extent should we delegate decisions to autonomous systems? The prediction of a human-free future is extreme—but serves as a warning.
