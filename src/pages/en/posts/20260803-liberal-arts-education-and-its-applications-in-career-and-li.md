In his academic journey, the author of the article on innig.net, titled 'What Liberal Arts Education Is for (2024)', decided to participate in a Religious Studies class called 'The Letters of Paul'. He chose this course for two reasons: his name is Paul and the professor, Cal Roetzel, was known for his innovative teaching methods.

The class was not considered practical; it was for fun, challenge, and acquiring cultural knowledge. However, the experience turned out to be more profound than expected. During the semester, they studied the Epistle to the Romans, progressing at a rate of about three sentences per week. This was because they analyzed various translations and commentaries of those sentences over centuries, as well as social and historical context.

The core of the course revolved around the question: what can we learn about what other people are thinking, about their mental models of the world, by paying close attention to the words they use?

Initially, the author did not see a connection between this course and his career as a software developer. However, when faced with confusing and potentially contradictory texts in his profession, he realized the value of the skills acquired. The task of interpreting the author's intent and resolving ambiguities became a valuable tool to avoid misunderstandings and hidden assumptions.

The skills of close reading and critical questioning, honed in a Religious Studies course, were effectively applied in software projects, saving resources and avoiding problems. The author of the text emphasizes that liberal arts education is not what is generally thought. Preparation for the future is uncertain, and often the most valuable skills are those that allow us to face an unknown and indescribable future.

Liberal arts education is not just about humanities and fine arts, but an approach that fosters curiosity, exploration, and openness to various perspectives. The author teaches computer science courses within this perspective, preparing students for entire lives, in ways that neither they nor the educator can predict at the moment.

The distinction between practical education and education of indescribable value is a recurring theme in education. The former, often called 'vocational education', focuses on specific knowledge for defined future situations. The latter, liberal arts education, aims to prepare the student for an uncertain future, where they can adapt and contribute to shaping the world.

The term 'liberal arts' has a history dating back to the Latin phrase 'artes liberalis', referring to 'skills or principles practiced worthy of a free person'. Liberal arts education is philosophical and attitudinal, not a set of specific disciplines.

The author concludes that, despite existing tensions in education, liberal arts education should be seen as preparation for an unknown future, where students will be helping to shape the world in which they will live.
