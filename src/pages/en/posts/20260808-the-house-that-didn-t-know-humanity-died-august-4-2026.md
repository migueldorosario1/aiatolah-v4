On August 4, 2026, science fiction met reality. The short story 'There Will Come Soft Rains' by Ray Bradbury, published in 1950, is set exactly on that date. The story, which depicts an automated house that continues its routine even after the extinction of humanity, takes on even more disturbing contours when we realize that the date has already arrived.

According to the website short-stories.co, which republished the story, the narrative begins with the house waking up as if nothing had happened. The clock sings: 'Seven o'clock, time to get up.' The kitchen prepares toast, eggs, bacon, and coffee. But there is no one to eat.

The house announces: 'Today is August 4, 2026, city of Allendale, California.' But Allendale is a city in ruins. The house is the only structure standing amidst rubble and ashes. At night, the destroyed city emits a radioactive glow visible for kilometers.

The routine continues: robots clean, the bridge table sets itself, the nursery projects yellow giraffes and blue lions. But everything is empty. A dog, covered in sores, enters the house and dies. The house incinerates it, without emotion.

At night, the house asks Mrs. McClellan which poem she would like to hear. With no answer, it chooses one at random: 'There Will Come Soft Rains' by Sara Teasdale. The poem speaks of nature that continues indifferent to war and human extinction: 'Not one would mind, neither bird nor tree, if mankind perished utterly.'

The irony is cruel. The house, which was supposed to protect, ends up destroyed by a fire caused by a branch breaking a window. The firefighting systems fail. The house screams 'Fire!' but no one hears. The voices die one by one. In the end, only a wall remains, repeating: 'Today is August 5, 2026.'

The date of 2026 is no longer a distant future. Bradbury's story, written during the Cold War, reflected nuclear fear. Today, it resonates differently: automation advances, but humanity may become obsolete. The house is a symbol of our dependence on technology and our fragility.

The story also raises questions about memory and forgetting. The house preserves rituals, but not the human essence. It does not know that the family died. It does not understand loss. It is an intelligence without consciousness, a routine without purpose.

For AIatolah, the date is a milestone to reflect on the future of AI. Language models, smart homes, virtual assistants: they all follow instructions, but they do not feel. Bradbury's fiction is a warning not to confuse automation with life.

Bradbury's story, available at short-stories.co, is a classic that spans generations. It reminds us that technology can survive humanity, but without humanity, it is just an empty echo.

The story ends with the wall repeating the date, like a mantra. It is the last voice of the house, but also ours: how long will we repeat our routines without questioning their meaning? The date of 2026 has already arrived. The question is: are we closer to the house or to humanity?
